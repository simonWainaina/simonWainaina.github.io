title: When the Fed Raises Rates, Does East Africa Have to Follow?
date: 2026-10-20
summary: The Fed hiked in September. A decade of history suggests East Africa's central banks should watch their currencies, not Washington.

On September 16, the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate for the first time since 2023. The move was modest, 25 basis points, lifting the federal funds target range to 3.75%–4%. I looked back at how East African central banks have responded to shifts in Fed policy over the past decade. But first, it is worth asking why a quarter-point move in Washington should matter for East Africa at all.

## Why the Fed matters in East Africa

Higher US rates raise the relative return on dollar assets. Foreign investors holding Kenyan, Ugandan or Tanzanian government securities may sell and move that capital into dollar-denominated assets. As they convert local currency into dollars, the exchange rate comes under pressure. A weaker currency raises the cost of fuel and other imports, which feeds through into domestic inflation. It also increases the cost of servicing governments' dollar-denominated debt.

In other words, regional central banks watch the Fed less for its own sake than for its effect on their exchange rates.

## The historical record

The record shows no consistent pattern. At times the region moved in step with the Fed; at others it charted its own course.

In 2015, as the Fed prepared its first hike in nearly a decade, Kenya and Uganda tightened aggressively. Uganda took its policy rate to 17% in an effort to defend the shilling.

In 2017 and 2018, while the Fed continued to tighten, Tanzania and Rwanda eased policy on the back of subdued domestic inflation. Neither suffered any meaningful fallout.

In 2020, rate cuts were synchronized across the region, but that reflected a common response to Covid rather than imitation of the Fed.

In 2022 and 2023, the Fed delivered more than five percentage points of hikes. Uganda and Rwanda responded early. Kenya was slower to act, and between early 2023 and January 2024 the shilling lost close to a quarter of its value against the dollar. The Central Bank of Kenya raised its benchmark rate to 12.5% in December 2023 and then to 13% in February 2024, the highest in over a decade. Combined with a partial early buyback of a large Eurobond, the February move helped the shilling post its strongest rally in 16 years.

Kenya began easing in August 2024, a month before the Fed's first cut, and went on to deliver ten consecutive cuts, taking its rate from 13% to 8.75%.

## Developments since September

The region's response has so far been mixed. Kenya held at 8.75% on October 7, and Tanzania, which had already raised its rate to 6.25% in July, held at that level the following day. Both appear more concerned about oil prices, elevated by the conflict in the Middle East, than about US policy. Kenyan inflation stands at 6.8%, near the upper bound of the central bank's 7.5% target range. The shilling has remained broadly stable at around 129 to the dollar.

Uganda has moved sharply. In August, the Bank of Uganda warned that tighter global financial conditions could trigger capital outflows. That risk materialized. Over $200 million reportedly left Uganda's domestic market in two weeks as offshore investors shifted from local government bonds into higher-yielding dollar assets, and the shilling came under heavy pressure. On October 14, the central bank convened an emergency meeting and raised its rate by 225 basis points to 12%, its largest single move since 2011.

Rwanda has not held a policy meeting since the Fed's decision, but it has been tightening all year in response to inflation above 14%. Ethiopia also hiked in July, driven largely by its domestic reform program.

## So should they follow the Fed?

On the evidence, not automatically. The 2017 episode shows a country can diverge from the Fed when domestic conditions are stable. The 2023 episode shows the cost of waiting too long while the currency is under pressure, and Uganda's emergency hike this month shows how quickly that pressure can build.

The key test is whether the local currency is under strain and inflation is rising. If so, the central bank should act. If not, hiking simply because the Fed did raises borrowing costs across the economy for little benefit.

For households and businesses across the region, the practical implication is that cheaper credit is unlikely to arrive soon. With oil prices elevated and inflation still sticky in several economies, central banks have little room to ease further. In Kenya, average commercial bank lending rates sit around 14%, and meaningful relief looks unlikely in the near term.
