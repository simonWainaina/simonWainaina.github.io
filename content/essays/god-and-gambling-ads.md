title: Should God Be Part of a Gambling Advertisement?
date: 2026-07-02
summary: A 4 a.m. Christian radio show, a premium-rate lottery called Cheza Box, and why faith shouldn't be a marketing tool.

This morning, I woke up at exactly 4:00 a.m. Normally, I wake up at 7:00 or 8:00 a.m., so it did not feel right to get out of bed at 4:00 a.m. I also did not want to start using my laptop or phone at that hour. I almost picked up a book and started reading, but I stopped myself. Then I remembered that I grew up listening to the radio, so I decided to listen to Radio Citizen online.

As was the case when I was living in Baringo, Kenya, a few years ago, the morning show was Christian-themed. The radio host, however, was different from the one I used to listen to while preparing to walk to school at around 4:00 or 5:00 a.m. on weekdays.

The host, a lady, did a good job selecting music to start the morning. She also did a good job of steering the conversation toward the Bible and Christian teachings, while using the everyday Kenyan Swahili that people naturally speak rather than the more formal version.

Throughout her presentation, however, perhaps more often than she talked about anything else, she kept encouraging listeners to try their luck by playing "Cheza Box."

Cheza Box is a type of radio premium lottery owned and advertised by many local radio stations in Kenya. In these games, listeners send a specified amount of money (usually at least KSh100) to enter a draw, after which one winner is selected, presumably at random. This means that each individual's chance of winning depends on how many people enter that particular draw. Meanwhile, the radio station collects money from every entry before paying out the prize, with telecom providers and the backend operators also taking a share. The secret to the business is simple: siri ni numbers 😂. The more people who play, and the more times they play, the more money the system generates.

What surprised me was not that the station advertised gambling, but that it repeatedly used Christian language to encourage listeners to gamble. To me, this raises an ethical question. Gambling is a commercial product whose business model depends on encouraging as many people as possible to keep playing. Faith, on the other hand, is deeply personal and is built on trust in God. When religious language is used to persuade people to spend money on a gambling product, the line between spiritual encouragement and commercial persuasion becomes blurred. Given that Christianity is the majority religion in Kenya and rural Kenya has higher radio listenership than urban Kenya (CAK, 2026), it is reasonable to worry that some listeners may interpret "have faith" as more than just a marketing slogan. I do not think religious language should be used to sell products whose primary purpose is commercial gain, especially products that carry a risk of financial harm.

While I understand that radio stations are for-profit organizations, I would hope that listeners are told what their chances of winning are and that, in gambling, they may never win. I am not arguing that radio stations should stop advertising or even stop running these games. They are businesses, and I understand that. My concern is much narrower: faith should not be used as a marketing tool for gambling. If these games are worth promoting, they should be promoted on their own merits, not by appealing to people's relationship with God.
